Can AI assess risks to public data?

Federal datasets can be altered, delayed, defunded, or quietly taken offline. Assessing that risk is skilled human work. This exercise tests whether AI agents can do it too — by having people and AI assess the same datasets against the same rubric, and comparing the results.

What we're testing

Two things, in order:

  1. The hypothesis. Can an AI agent, given a rubric written by data policy experts, produce a risk assessment comparable to a human researcher's? This is research instrumentation, not a teaching tool.
  2. The prompts. When people steer an AI toward better assessments, how they steer it tells us how to build a better automated monitoring tool. Your transcripts are the data.

This exercise comes out of the Public Data Project's data monitoring work and sits alongside Binoc, a tool that generates changelogs for datasets that don't have them. Binoc watches what has changed; this exercise probes whether AI can anticipate what might.

The three phases

Async or sync

1 · Human research

You research a federal dataset's ecosystem — no AI allowed — and rate its risk against the rubric. About 50 minutes.

2 · Automated benchmark

Ahead of your session, our harness gives an AI agent the same dataset and rubric with zero human steering, and logs its reasoning.

How it works
Sync sessions only

3 · Human-prompted AI

You run the assessment through an AI agent of your choice, coaching its method — never its answers. About 50 minutes.

Who this is for

People with domain judgment about public data: data rescue groups, data librarians, policy researchers, journalists. You do not need AI experience — the instructions assume none.

One rule above all

Do not consult America's Data Index until the end of the exercise. It's the closest thing to an answer key, and looking early contaminates the comparison. You'll be pointed to it at the finish.
